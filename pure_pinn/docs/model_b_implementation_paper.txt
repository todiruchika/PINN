A HARD-CONSTRAINED PHYSICS-ONLY PINN FOR SPARSE-SENSOR THERMAL
RECONSTRUCTION OF MULTICORE CHIPS: IMPLEMENTATION AND EVALUATION
================================================================================

Siya Patil, Ruchika Todi, Smriti Prashanth Vasisht, Vipul Reddy Gundalu,
Rashmi R (B.M.S. College of Engineering, Bengaluru)
Mallanagouda Patil (R V Institute of Technology and Management, Bengaluru)

Draft implementation report -- component B of a three-model comparative study.
Status: Model B complete and evaluated; Models A and C pending.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ABSTRACT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We report the implementation and evaluation of a physics-only physics-informed
neural network (PINN) that reconstructs the full 128x128 steady-state
temperature field of a 2-D multicore chip from only 5 to 10 sparse point-sensor
readings. The model uses no training dataset: each chip is solved from scratch
using its own sensors, its metadata constants, and the steady-state heat
equation. Boundary conditions are enforced exactly by construction rather than
by penalty, and the unknown heat-source field is inferred jointly with the
temperature field. On held-out test chips the model attains a relative L2 field
error of 0.124-0.135 with a boundary error of exactly zero, and localises
hotspots to within 0.06-0.07 of the domain width. An oracle-source diagnostic
shows that roughly two thirds of the residual error is attributable to
under-determination of the heat source by sparse sensors rather than to the
network's representational capacity. This component establishes the physics and
evaluation code subsequently reused by the hybrid model.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. INTRODUCTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Thermal management of multicore processors requires knowledge of the full
on-die temperature field, but instrumented silicon provides only a handful of
point measurements. Reconstructing the field from such sparse data is an
ill-posed inverse problem: infinitely many temperature fields pass through five
measurements.

Three modelling paradigms are compared in the wider study: a purely
data-driven baseline trained across many chips (Model A), a physics-only PINN
solved per chip (Model B), and a hybrid that adds a physics residual to the
data-driven model (Model C). This report covers Model B.

Model B is deliberately the most information-starved of the three. It sees no
other chip, no training set, and no ground-truth field. Its only inputs are the
sparse sensors of the chip in question, two per-chip physical constants, and
the governing equation. It is therefore not a predictor that generalises but a
solver that is re-run per instance -- an asymmetry that is itself one of the
study's findings, since it trades a single amortised training cost for a
recurring per-chip cost.

The contributions of this component are:

  (i)   an exactly-enforced, non-zero, per-chip Dirichlet boundary condition
        imposed by construction, requiring no boundary loss weight;
  (ii)  joint inference of the unknown heat-source field alongside the
        temperature field, with three interchangeable source parametrisations;
  (iii) a verified, reusable implementation of the strong-form residual, shared
        unchanged with the hybrid model so that the three-way comparison
        measures modelling paradigm rather than incidental coding differences;
  (iv)  an oracle-source diagnostic that separates inverse-problem error from
        representation error -- a decomposition rarely reported in the PINN
        literature.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. METHODOLOGY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

2.1 Problem statement and dataset
---------------------------------
The steady-state temperature field T(x, y) on the normalised chip domain
Omega = [0,1]^2 satisfies the Poisson form of the heat conduction equation

        -k * laplacian(T) = Q(x, y)        in Omega                        (1)
         T = T_boundary                    on dOmega                       (2)

where k is thermal conductivity and Q is the volumetric power generated by
active logic blocks. The dataset provides 500 training, 100 validation and 100
test chips as 128x128 finite-element fields with 5-, 8- and 10-sensor readouts
and a metadata index.

Four dataset properties were verified numerically before any modelling, and
three of them contradicted the initial study assumptions:

  * k is per-chip, uniform in [100, 150] W/(m K) -- not shared across chips.
  * T_boundary is per-chip in [20, 40] degC -- never zero. It matches the
    field's border values to 3e-7 degC and its min_temperature to 5e-5 degC.
  * Heat sources are axis-aligned rectangles of uniform power density (1-10 per
    chip, up to 2.5e7 W/m^2) -- not Gaussian blobs.
  * Field matrices are indexed M[i, j] = T(x = j/127, y = i/127), i.e. the row
    index is y; sensors coincide exactly with grid nodes.

Substituting the metadata into (1) and evaluating a five-point finite-difference
Laplacian on the provided fields reproduces Q to within 2-5% mean relative
residual, the discrepancy being consistent with staircasing of rectangle edges
on a 128-point grid. The governing equation and its sign convention are
therefore confirmed.

2.2 Non-dimensionalisation
-------------------------
Peak temperatures span 66 to 15153 degC across chips, which is poorly
conditioned for neural network training. Each chip is solved in the variables

        T_norm = (T - T_boundary) / s,      f_norm = Q / (k * s)           (3)

with s = (hottest sensor reading) - T_boundary, so that (1)-(2) become

        laplacian(T_norm) + f_norm = 0,     T_norm = 0 on dOmega           (4)

Crucially s is computed from the sensor readings and metadata alone, never from
the ground-truth field, so no evaluation signal enters training. All reported
metrics are computed after inverting (3), in degrees Celsius.

2.3 Exact boundary enforcement
------------------------------
The temperature field is represented as

        T_norm(x, y) = d(x, y) * NN(x, y),  d = 16 x(1-x) y(1-y)           (5)

Because d vanishes identically on all four edges, (5) satisfies the Dirichlet
condition exactly for every parameter value, and hence at every training step
and for every chip. In physical units T = T_boundary + s * d * NN, i.e. the
network predicts only the rise above ambient and that rise is forced to vanish
at the boundary. The factor 16 normalises max d = 1 so the network output need
not be large to reach realistic temperatures.

This removes the boundary loss term and its weight from the problem entirely.
Note that a non-zero boundary is no obstacle: the construction requires the
boundary value to be *known*, not zero. Note also that hard enforcement applies
only to the boundary condition; the governing equation is necessarily enforced
softly, through the residual, since a network satisfying (1) identically would
amount to a closed-form solution.

Two ablation arms are implemented for comparison: a soft arm, in which the
network predicts temperature directly and the boundary is imposed by a penalty
term at sampled edge points, and a free arm, which receives no boundary
information at all and must recover it from the residual and interior sensors.

2.4 Source parametrisation
--------------------------
Q is unknown and is inferred jointly with the field. Three parametrisations,
all constrained non-negative, are implemented:

  gaussian   6 isotropic Gaussian blobs with trainable amplitude, centre and
             width (24 parameters). Amplitudes are exponential so they may
             traverse orders of magnitude; centres and widths pass through
             bounded sigmoids.
  softrect   smoothed axis-aligned rectangles (trainable centre, half-extent,
             amplitude), matching the dataset's true generative form.
  mlp        a second small network with softplus output; no shape assumption.

2.5 Loss and optimisation
-------------------------
The objective contains no ground-truth term:

        L = w_r * L_res + w_d * L_data                                     (6)
        L_res  = mean over collocation points of (laplacian(T_norm)+f_norm)^2
        L_data = mean squared error at the N sensor locations

Second derivatives are obtained by two applications of reverse-mode automatic
differentiation to the network's own output, so the residual is evaluated
without any discretisation stencil or reference solution. The default weights
are w_r = 1, w_d = 100; a gradient-norm annealing scheme is also implemented as
an alternative to fixed weights.

Optimisation proceeds in two phases: 5000 Adam steps (learning rate 1e-3 with
cosine decay to 1e-5, 2048 collocation points resampled every step), followed
by 2000 L-BFGS steps with strong-Wolfe line search on a frozen set of 4096
points, since resampling invalidates the line search. Because L-BFGS is
observed to step uphill on this objective, the best iterate by total loss is
retained. The field network is a 4x64 tanh MLP (12737 parameters); with the
Gaussian source the total is 12761 trainable parameters.

2.6 Evaluation protocol
-----------------------
Model B never uses the train or validation splits, so there is no
generalisation gap to measure. "Testing" means repeating the identical
from-scratch solve on each held-out test chip and reporting the distribution of
errors. After training, the field is evaluated on the full 128x128 grid,
de-normalised, and only then compared with the finite-element field. Metrics:
relative L2 field error (primary); maximum absolute error (secondary, since
hotspot misses matter more than mean error for reliability); peak-location
error; boundary MAE; sensor RMSE; error of the inferred source against the
metadata hotspots; and wall-clock cost. Every configuration is repeated over
multiple seeds and reported as mean +/- standard deviation.

2.7 Implementation and verification
-----------------------------------
The code is organised so that the physics is written and validated once:
data_loader.py (loading, scaling, splits), losses.py (Laplacian, residual, data
and boundary losses, collocation sampling), models/pinn.py (field, hard-BC
construction, source parametrisations), train_pinn.py (per-chip driver),
evaluate.py (metrics, aggregation, plots). losses.py and evaluate.py are reused
unchanged by the hybrid model.

A test suite guards the physics: the autodiff Laplacian is checked against an
analytic result; the residual is checked to vanish on a manufactured solution;
the hard-BC construction is checked to be exactly zero on sampled boundary
points; the dataset's own fields are checked to satisfy the normalised equation;
and the sensor/matrix indexing convention is checked. All eight checks pass.

Runs are bit-reproducible. Seeding covers the Python, NumPy and Torch
generators, and re-solving the nine (chip, sensor count) combinations at seed 0
in a separate invocation reproduced the original relative L2 errors exactly:
maximum absolute difference 0.0 across all nine pairs. Reported figures
therefore correspond exactly to the tabulated runs.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. RESULTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

3.1 Calibration
---------------
Loss weighting and source parametrisation were selected on a single held-out
chip (thermal_00601, N = 5, seed 0, reduced budget of 3000 Adam + 1000 L-BFGS
steps). Loss balancing:

        balancing            rel L2    max abs err    sensor fit L_data
        w_data = 1           0.317        1292 degC        2.0e-03
        annealing            0.325        1326 degC        3.7e-06
        w_data = 100         0.295        1245 degC        1.7e-06
        w_data = 1e4         0.290        1217 degC        3.4e-07

w_data = 100 was adopted: it saturates the sensor fit without an extreme
weight, and gradient-norm annealing conferred no advantage. Source
parametrisation:

        source                        rel L2   source rel L2   centre err
        gaussian, 6 blobs             0.275        0.851          0.086
        gaussian, true count (4)      0.283        0.854          0.077
        gaussian, 10 blobs            0.302        0.865          0.134
        gaussian 6, wider field net   0.290        0.863          0.088
        mlp (free-form)               0.384        0.911            --
        softrect                      0.414        0.922          0.231

Two observations are of independent interest. First, the correctly specified
source model performs worst: softrect matches the dataset's true rectangular
generative form, yet its sharp edges render the inverse problem markedly harder
to optimise, so the mis-specified but smooth Gaussian wins. Model-form
correctness and trainability are in tension here. Second, supplying the true
number of hotspots does not help (0.283 versus 0.275), so no oracle-count
variant is required in the main comparison.

3.2 Main results: sensor density
--------------------------------
Three test chips (thermal_00652, 00664, 00684; 8, 2 and 3 hotspots; peak
temperatures 4340, 470 and 2957 degC) at each sensor count and two seeds, 18
solves at the default configuration. The +/- figure is the standard deviation
across seeds, having first averaged over chips.

        N     rel L2          max abs err   peak loc err   boundary MAE
        5     0.135 +/- 0.002    558 degC       0.075         0.0
        8     0.124 +/- 0.004    430 degC       0.064         0.0
        10    0.126 +/- 0.002    478 degC       0.060         0.0

Chip-to-chip spread is far larger than seed spread (standard deviation
0.037-0.051; range 0.068-0.201 at N = 5), so the identity of the chip matters
much more than the random seed. Per-chip mean errors were 0.126, 0.141 and
0.118 for the three chips respectively.

Increasing the sensor count from 5 to 10 reduces the error only from 0.135 to
0.126, which is well within chip-to-chip spread. For a physics-only model the
governing equation, not the data, supplies most of the constraint; additional
randomly placed sensors mostly add redundant readings in cool regions. Sensor
placement, not sensor count, is the binding limitation.

The boundary MAE is exactly zero in all 18 runs, with no boundary term in the
objective, confirming the construction of Section 2.3.

Qualitatively (see results/figures/), the reconstruction is visually close to
the finite-element field: at the best-performing configuration
(thermal_00684, N = 10, rel L2 = 0.071) the maximum error is 355 degC against a
peak of 2957 degC, and the residual error appears as two smooth lobes flanking
the hot region -- the signature of a smooth source approximating a rectangular
one -- while decaying to zero at all four edges.

3.3 Error decomposition
-----------------------
An oracle diagnostic isolates representational error by supplying the true
rectangular source and training on the residual alone, with no sensor data. On
thermal_00684 this floor is 0.038 against an actual Model B error of 0.118;
hence roughly two thirds of the error arises from under-determination of the
source by sparse sensors rather than from the network's capacity. On the harder
thermal_00601 the floor is 0.086.

The floor is not a training-budget artefact. Doubling Adam steps, tripling
L-BFGS steps and quadrupling collocation points (2048 to 8192) reduced the
final residual threefold (2.08 to 0.68) while leaving the field error unchanged
at 0.038. Additional optimisation therefore buys nothing; the residual gap
reflects the smooth network's difficulty in reproducing the sharp curvature
induced by rectangular sources -- a manifestation of spectral bias.

3.4 Hotspot recovery
--------------------
Averaged over all runs the predicted peak is 0.90 of the true peak, i.e. the
hottest point is systematically under-predicted by about 10% and by up to 28%
in the worst case. This correlates with sensor coverage of the hot region
(correlation 0.63 between peak recovery and the ratio of the hottest sensor
reading to the true peak): where a sensor happens to fall on the hot region,
the peak is recovered to within a few percent; where every sensor misses it,
recovery degrades to 0.72-0.75. The physics constrains curvature relative to
source strength, but only a measurement near the peak distinguishes a dim broad
source from a bright narrow one.

The inferred source recovers hotspot *locations* well (centre error 0.11-0.15)
while the source field itself remains poor (relative error about 0.85). The
defensible claim is therefore localisation of hotspots, not recovery of the
power distribution.

3.5 Cost
--------
A single solve takes approximately 816 s (mean over the 18 runs) on a 12-core
CPU-only machine with four solves running concurrently, against millisecond
inference for an amortised data-driven model. Wall-clock figures are comparable
only at equal concurrency. Extrapolated, the full test split at three sensor
counts and three seeds (900 solves) would require of order 50 hours, which is
why the Model B arm is evaluated on a deterministically subsampled set of test
chips, stated explicitly rather than silently.

3.6 Limitations
---------------
Calibration (Section 3.1) used a held-out test chip rather than a validation
chip; repeating it on the validation split is outstanding. The main results
cover 3 chips and 2 seeds, not the intended 20 chips and 3 seeds. The soft and
free boundary ablation arms are implemented but not yet run. The hard-BC
construction exploits the square geometry; a non-convex die would require a
distance function that is considerably harder to construct.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. CONCLUSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A physics-only PINN reconstructs the full thermal field of a multicore chip
from as few as five point measurements at a relative L2 error of about 0.13,
with hotspot locations recovered to a few percent of the domain width and the
boundary condition satisfied exactly by construction, without any boundary
weight to tune. The implementation is verified against analytic and
manufactured solutions and against the dataset's own fields, and its physics
and evaluation modules are reused unchanged by the hybrid model, so the wider
three-way comparison measures paradigm rather than implementation.

Three findings are of interest beyond this component. Sensor count is
comparatively unimportant for a physics-only reconstruction, while sensor
placement relative to the hotspots governs whether peak magnitude is
recoverable at all. The correctly specified source model is outperformed by a
smoother, mis-specified one, indicating that optimisability can dominate
model-form fidelity in inverse PINN problems. And the oracle decomposition
shows that the dominant error is informational rather than representational,
which predicts that a hybrid model -- able to import a prior over plausible
source layouts from other chips -- should improve specifically on the component
that the physics alone cannot supply.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. FUTURE SCOPE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Anisotropic sources. The true heaters are elongated rectangles; isotropic
   Gaussians cannot match their aspect ratio, while sharp-edged rectangles are
   hard to optimise. Elliptical Gaussians with separate x and y widths preserve
   smoothness while adding aspect ratio, and are the most promising immediate
   improvement.

2. Curriculum on source sharpness. Beginning with smooth blobs and gradually
   sharpening towards rectangles may recover the correct generative form
   without the optimisation difficulty observed in Section 3.1.

3. Spectral remedies for the floor. Since the floor is representational rather
   than budget-limited, Fourier feature embeddings or adaptive collocation
   refinement near hot regions are the appropriate levers.

4. Sensor placement as a design variable. Given that placement dominates count,
   optimising where sensors are located -- rather than adding more -- is both a
   stronger scientific result and a directly actionable engineering one.

5. Completion of the comparison. Running the full experiment matrix, the two
   boundary ablation arms, and Models A and C, with the reporting protocol
   (baseline effort, hardware, concurrency, seed variance, error definition)
   stated alongside every number.

6. Geometry generalisation. Extending exact boundary enforcement to non-square
   dies via approximate distance functions, which would test whether the
   accuracy advantage of hard enforcement survives realistic floorplans.
